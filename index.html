A 100-person furniture company can be AI-assisted in 90 days, and the first thing to change is not the paper.

The question was how to lead a small company through the move to AI so revenue grows. This is my answer, worked through on a furniture maker. Paper goes away as a result of fixing where money is lost, not as step one.

    Start from where revenue leaks

    Removing paper feels like progress, but it only digitises the old process. I would begin with a different question: where does this company lose money or growth each week? In a furniture business, it is almost always one of three places.

    • Slow answers. Quotes and inquiries wait for someone who is in the workshop or on the floor. Speed of reply is one of the strongest predictors of who wins the order.
    • Re-keying. The same order is typed from email to spreadsheet to production sheet to invoice. Each copy is a chance for an error, and errors become remakes and returns.
    • Blind spots. Lead times, stock and customer history live in people’s heads, so planning and follow-up are guesses.

    AI helps with all three because they are reading, writing and lookup problems. Going paperless then happens as a side effect: once a workflow is digital and assisted, the paper step has no reason to exist, and the people using it are the ones who retire it.

    The 90-day plan

    Pick a phase to see what happens, who is involved, and what has to be true before moving on.

    Bringing people with you

    Adoption fails more often than the technology does. The plan loops in the top, the middle and the people doing the work from the first week.

    Owner and leadership

    Choose one number the program must move, such as quote response time or on-time delivery. Say plainly what AI is for here (growth and less grunt work) and what it is not. Use the tools themselves, visibly.

    Department leads

    Each lead owns one workflow in their area, with a champion on their team. They decide which manual steps stop, so change is not handed down from outside.

    People doing the work

    Ask them which task they dislike most. Start the pilot there. They are the first users, they review every output, and their feedback changes the tool every week.

    A small AI council of five or six people from sales, shop floor, finance and leadership meets for 30 minutes a month. It reviews results, approves new use cases and hears concerns. It keeps the program from becoming one person’s project.

      Guardrails that keep it safe

      A small company does not need a compliance department. It needs five habits, set up before the first pilot.

      A human approves anything customer-facing or involving money
      AI drafts, a person sends. Approval can be loosened later for a workflow that has earned it.
      Test on real history before going live
      Run 50 past quotes through the assistant and compare with what was actually sent. Track accuracy weekly, and track how often people edit the draft.
      Simple data rules
      Green: product and price data. Amber: customer names and order history, business-tier tools only. Red: payment data and HR files stay out.
      Keep a log
      Every AI-generated output is stored with its inputs, so an error can be traced and fixed rather than argued about.
      Keep the old way available for 30 days
      Retire the paper step only when the new one has held up. Rollback is one decision, not a project.

      From one company to sixteen

      The role posting describes working with the C-suites of 16 wholly owned portfolio companies. This plan is built to repeat: the 90 days, the people layers, the guardrails and the scorecard stay the same, and only the first workflow changes. Each company that finishes adds to a shared library of tested prompts, evaluation sets and integration code, so the next one starts further ahead.
